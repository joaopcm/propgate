# Keys, policies, and trees

import { Callout } from "@/components/mdx/callout";

Four more record types. The pattern from SPF holds throughout: parse the record
into what it means, then reason about the meaning. What changes is what each one
means.

## A DKIM record is a key, not a string

A DKIM record is a tag list, and the tag that matters is `p=`: the public half
of the signing key, base64-encoded.

Parsing it as a key rather than comparing it as text buys three things a string
comparison cannot have. You learn the algorithm and the key length. You can tell
"a different key" from "the same key, split differently". And you can tell
either of those from "not a key at all".

<Lookup
  name="short._domainkey.dkim.test"
  notice={
    <>
      <p>
        A real 512-bit RSA key. It parses, it is syntactically perfect, and
        receivers are already rejecting signatures made with it. Nothing about
        the record text says so: the length is a property of the parsed key.
      </p>
      <p>
        Compare <code>ed25519._domainkey.dkim.test</code>, which is 32 raw bytes
        rather than an SPKI wrapper (RFC 8463). A parser that assumes RSA
        structure rejects a perfectly good key.
      </p>
    </>
  }
  server="auth"
  shape="A valid record whose key is too short to be trusted."
  type="TXT"
/>

## Empty p= means revoked

RFC 6376 §3.6.1: an empty `p=` is not a truncated record. It is revocation. The
domain owner is saying this selector's key is no longer valid.

Reporting it as malformed tells somebody to go and fix a record they broke on
purpose.

<Lookup
  name="revoked._domainkey.dkim.test"
  notice={
    <>
      <p>
        <code>v=DKIM1; k=rsa; p=</code>: complete, correct, and deliberate.
      </p>
      <p>
        Two more worth reading in the same zone. <code>t=y</code> on{" "}
        <code>testing._domainkey</code> means receivers must ignore signature
        failures, so the key is published and protecting nothing yet, which is
        a warning, not a pass. And <code>vlast._domainkey</code> puts{" "}
        <code>v=</code> second, which the RFC says MUST be first.
      </p>
    </>
  }
  server="auth"
  shape="A record whose p= tag has no value."
  type="TXT"
/>

## Base64 is case-sensitive and DNS names are not

Two facts, each unremarkable, which combine into a trap.

DNS names fold case. `S6._DomainKey.example.com` and
`s6._domainkey.example.com` are the same name, and a query for either finds a
record published as the other.

Base64 does not fold case. `MIIBIjAN` and `miibijan` are different byte
sequences, and only one of them is the key you issued.

So a comparison that lowercases the whole record to be safe about the name has
just made two different keys look identical.

<Lookup
  name="s5._domainkey.txt-split.test"
  notice={
    <>
      <p>
        This key differs from the expected value only in case. It must{" "}
        <em>not</em> be treated as a match, and a case-insensitive comparison
        does exactly that.
      </p>
      <p>
        The other direction is <code>S6._DomainKey.txt-split.test</code>,
        published with capitals in the owner name and which a lowercase query
        must still find. Both behaviours are needed at once, on the same
        comparison, for opposite reasons.
      </p>
    </>
  }
  server="auth"
  shape="A valid-looking key that is the expected one lowercased."
  type="TXT"
/>

## DMARC lives at the organizational domain

DMARC is published at `_dmarc.<domain>`, and RFC 7489 §6.6.3 defines discovery
in two steps. A receiver queries the exact name first. Only if that returns
nothing does it fall back to the *organizational* domain.

The organizational domain is one label below the public suffix: `example.co.uk`
for `mail.example.co.uk`, since `co.uk` is a suffix and `.uk` alone is not. That
determination needs the Public Suffix List, which is why a DMARC evaluator ships
one.

The consequence people get backwards: `sp=` governs a subdomain **only when the
subdomain publishes nothing of its own.**

<Lookup
  name="_dmarc.own.dmarc.test"
  notice={
    <>
      <p>
        <code>p=none</code>. This subdomain publishes its own policy, so the
        exact-name lookup wins and the organizational <code>sp=quarantine</code>{" "}
        is irrelevant.
      </p>
      <p>
        Now query <code>_dmarc.inherits.dmarc.test</code>, which has nothing.
        Then query <code>_dmarc.dmarc.test</code> and read its{" "}
        <code>sp=</code>. That is the policy which applies to the subdomain:{" "}
        <code>quarantine</code>, not the <code>p=reject</code> next to it.
      </p>
    </>
  }
  server="auth"
  shape="A DMARC record at the subdomain itself."
  type="TXT"
/>

Two more DMARC facts that produce findings rather than booleans. `p=none` is
monitoring only: a valid record enforcing nothing, which is a warning rather
than a pass. And `pct=20` applies the policy to a fifth of mail, delivering the
rest regardless, which most people who set it do not realise.

<Callout>
  The same "discard non-matching records before counting" rule from SPF applies
  here. `_dmarc.shared.dmarc.test` has a DMARC record and a Google verification
  token at the same name. Counting TXT records reports it as ambiguous; counting
  DMARC records reports it correctly as one policy.
</Callout>

## Authorising someone else to receive your reports

`rua=mailto:reports@analytics-vendor.com` asks a third party to receive
aggregate reports about your domain. Left unchecked, anyone could nominate
anyone as a firehose destination.

RFC 7489 §7.1 closes that: the destination has to opt in by publishing
`<source>._report._dmarc.<destination>` containing a DMARC record.

<Lookup
  name="dmarc.test._report._dmarc.reports.test"
  notice={
    <>
      <p>
        <code>v=DMARC1</code>, and that is all it needs to say. The presence of
        the record at that name is the authorisation.
      </p>
      <p>
        Compare <code>unauth-reports.test</code>, which is named as a
        destination and publishes no such record. Reports sent there will be
        dropped by any receiver that checks, so the domain owner is getting
        fewer reports than they think, silently.
      </p>
    </>
  }
  server="auth"
  shape="A minimal DMARC record at the report-authorisation name."
  type="TXT"
/>

## The null MX

RFC 7505: an MX record with preference 0 and a single dot as the exchange means
this domain accepts no mail, deliberately, and says so.

Here is the thing that makes MX different from every other check in this
course. The same record is correct or catastrophic depending on what the domain
is *for*, and no amount of looking at DNS can tell you which.

A null MX on a send-only domain is exactly right. On a domain that receives
mail, every message bounces.

<Lookup
  name="nomail.mx.test"
  notice={
    <>
      <p>
        <code>0 .</code>: preference zero, exchange is the root. That is the
        whole record.
      </p>
      <p>
        Then look at <code>ambiguous.mx.test</code>, which has a null MX{" "}
        <em>and</em> a real exchange. RFC 7505 §3 forbids the pair and senders
        disagree about what it means, so delivery depends on whose mail server
        is trying. Nobody is misconfigured in a way you can point at, and
        delivery is a coin flip.
      </p>
    </>
  }
  server="auth"
  shape="MX preference 0 with a single dot as the exchange."
  type="MX"
/>

This is the observation that forces the next unit into existence. An evaluator
cannot decide this alone, so something has to tell it what the domain is for.

## An MX may not point at an alias

RFC 2181 §10.3 forbids it. Most senders follow the alias anyway, which is
exactly why the ones that refuse look intermittent rather than broken.

<Lookup
  name="aliased.mx.test"
  notice={
    <>
      <p>
        The exchange is <code>alias.mx.test</code>. Query that for a{" "}
        <code>CNAME</code> and you will find one. Forbidden, widely tolerated,
        and the source of "some of our mail gets through" tickets.
      </p>
      <p>
        Three more in the same zone. <code>literal.mx.test</code> holds an
        address where a name belongs: the zone file accepts it, dig prints it,
        and senders look it up as a name and find nothing.{" "}
        <code>dangling.mx.test</code> points at a name with no address of either
        family. And <code>sixth.mx.test</code> has only AAAA, which is unusual,
        legitimate, and must not be reported as missing an address.
      </p>
    </>
  }
  server="auth"
  shape="An MX whose exchange is itself an alias."
  type="MX"
/>

## Climbing the CAA tree

CAA says which certificate authorities may issue for a name. RFC 8659 defines
the lookup as a climb: start at the name, and if there is no CAA record set,
try the parent, and keep going up to but not including the root.

Two properties of that climb are easy to get wrong.

**The nearest ancestor with a CAA record set wins outright.** Policies are never
merged up the tree. A subdomain with its own CAA record completely replaces the
apex policy rather than adding to it.

**The Public Suffix List plays no part.** Unlike DMARC, this is a plain walk up
the name.

<Lookup
  name="host.sub.caa.test"
  notice={
    <>
      <p>
        Nothing here, so climb. Query <code>sub.caa.test</code> and you get{" "}
        <code>0 issue "digicert.com"</code>. That is the answer, and the apex
        policy naming letsencrypt is <em>not</em> merged in, so letsencrypt is
        not authorised at this name even though it is at the apex.
      </p>
      <p>
        Compare <code>deep.nested.caa.test</code>, which has no CAA of its own
        and no CAA at <code>nested.caa.test</code> either, so the climb goes two
        steps to the apex. An implementation that stops after one step gets this
        wrong.
      </p>
    </>
  }
  server="auth"
  shape="No CAA records, so the answer is somewhere above."
  type="CAA"
/>

Then the property semantics. `issue` governs ordinary certificates and
`issuewild` governs wildcards, and confusing them authorises the wrong CA for
the wrong kind of certificate. A value of `;` means no authority may issue at
all.

<Lookup
  name="critical.caa.test"
  notice={
    <>
      <p>
        Flag 128 (the critical bit) on a property no authority recognises.
        RFC 8659 §4.1 requires a CA that does not understand a critical property
        to refuse issuance, so this blocks everything <em>despite</em> also
        carrying a perfectly good <code>issue "letsencrypt.org"</code> next to
        it.
      </p>
      <p>
        Also worth reading: <code>params.caa.test</code>, whose value carries
        ACME account-binding parameters after a semicolon. Those must not be
        mistaken for part of the CA's name, or the authorised CA is a string
        nobody matches.
      </p>
    </>
  }
  server="auth"
  shape="Two CAA records, one of which has flag 128."
  type="CAA"
/>

<Callout kind="warning">
  In a zone file, an unquoted `;` starts a comment. `@ IN CAA 0 issue ;` is a
  deny-all that silently becomes empty rdata. The fixture zones quote it, and
  the reason is written next to it: this is the kind of detail that looks like a
  typo until you know.
</Callout>

Five record types now, each parsed rather than matched. What is missing is the
thing MX made unavoidable: somebody has to say what the domain is for.
