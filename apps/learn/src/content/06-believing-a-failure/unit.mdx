# Believing a failure

Everything up to here has been about one check, run once, answered honestly.

This unit is about the harder question, and the one with the highest stakes in
the whole product: a check just failed. Should anybody be told?

## The webhook that pages the wrong person

Picture the chain. You monitor a customer's domain. The customer is a platform.
Their customer is a business whose mail depends on it. You fire
`domain.failed`. Their alerting picks it up. Somebody's phone goes off at
three in the morning.

Now suppose the record was fine and one resolver had a bad second.

You have not delivered a slightly inaccurate result. You have woken somebody up
for nothing, and you have taught a platform that your notifications are noise.
A monitoring product that does that is worse than one that does not exist,
because at least the absent one leaves them polling their own DNS and trusting
what they see.

That is the constraint everything below is built to satisfy.

## Asking from more than one place

The first defence is not asking one resolver. Every authenticated check runs
against a pool of them concurrently.

The same pool for the interactive verify and for the background sweep,
deliberately: a verify that consulted one resolver and a sweep that consulted
another could disagree about the same domain a minute apart, and the customer
would have no way to tell which was right.

Concurrently rather than in sequence, so the wall clock is the slowest vantage
point rather than their sum. Three resolvers cost roughly 1.2 to 1.5 times one,
not three times, which is what makes consensus affordable on a button press.

## Disagreement is uncertainty, not failure

Reconciliation compares a signature per vantage point: the verdict plus the
sorted finding codes.

Deliberately not the raw records. Two resolvers legitimately return an RRset in
different orders and with different remaining TTLs, and calling that a
divergence would fire on every healthy domain, which is the failure mode this
whole codebase is most careful about.

Three outcomes:

**Unanimous.** The answer passes through untouched. Nothing to say.

**A strict majority.** The majority's verdict is kept, but raised to at least
`warn`, with a divergence finding attached. This is the point of having three
rather than two: one resolver serving a stale answer is outvoted rather than
being able to make the whole check uncertain. The divergence is still reported,
because a customer mid-propagation should be told that rather than left
wondering why the answer changed.

**No majority.** Two that disagree, or a three-way split. There is nothing to
believe, so the verdict is `indeterminate`, which is exactly the verdict unit 5
built for this.

<Callout>
  Every lookup from every vantage point is kept in the result, not just the
  winner's. A query made and not shown is a cost the caller pays and cannot see.
  When the question is "why do you say this is uncertain", the losing vantage
  point's lookups are the entire answer.
</Callout>

## What three resolvers on one host cannot see

The honest limit, stated because a product that overclaims here is selling
something it does not have.

Several resolvers reached from one machine share an egress IP. They are only
weakly independent. What they catch is real: cache state, propagation lag, one
resolver being broken.

What they cannot see is GeoDNS, anycast, or a network path that differs by
geography. A domain answering differently in Frankfurt than in São Paulo looks
identical from here.

The fixture tier has the same limitation and says so. Real GeoDNS is out of
reach from one host, so the divergence fixture is two authoritative servers
serving different content for the same name, which exercises the reconciliation
logic exactly, and is not a simulation of geography.

## Counting failures

Consensus handles one bad resolver. It does not handle a bad *minute*, when
every vantage point sees the same transient problem.

So the state machine counts. A single failing check does not move a verified
domain anywhere. It takes consecutive failures.

```
pending → verifying → verified → degraded → failed
                          ↑          |         |
                          └──────────┴─────────┘
                              recovered
```

The thresholds are one for `degraded` and three for `failed`, and both are
commented in the source as **unmeasured**, with the measurement that would
justify them named next to them.

That honesty is the point. One for a warning is the conservative choice: it
costs a possibly-noisy event and buys the shortest time to notice. Three for a
failure, at the five-minute cadence a degraded domain gets, is roughly ten
minutes of sustained failure before anyone says so: long enough to outlast a
resolver restart or a zone reload, short enough to be useful.

<Callout kind="warning">
  Neither number came from data. The receipt they are waiting on is the observed
  distribution of consecutive transient failures across real monitored domains
  over thirty days. A limit with no measurement behind it is a landmine, and
  labelling it as one is the least a codebase can do while it waits.
</Callout>

## Why indeterminate neither increments nor resets

This is the sharpest piece of reasoning in the implementation, and it is worth
working through rather than reading.

A check comes back `indeterminate`. What happens to the failure counter?

**Reset it to zero?** Then a domain that alternates failure and unreachability
never accumulates three consecutive failures. It can never reach `failed`. A
genuinely broken domain behind a flaky resolver would be monitored forever and
never reported. That is a real bug hiding inside an intuitive choice.

**Increment it?** Then our own resolver having a bad minute pages somebody.
Which is the thing this entire unit exists to prevent.

**Leave it exactly as it was.** Uncertainty is not evidence of failure, and it is
not evidence of health either. Nothing moves and the counter is untouched.

Three options, two of which are wrong in opposite directions and both of which
look reasonable in isolation.

## degraded needs something to have regressed from

A second subtlety with the same flavour.

`degraded` means "this used to work and now does not". So it needs something to
have regressed from. A domain nobody has ever verified cannot have got worse.

Without that guard, two things fire `domain.degraded` when they should not: a
freshly registered domain whose first check finds nothing published, and a
domain reset to `pending` because its expectations were rotated. Both would be a
notification saying "this used to work" about something that never did.

So a failing domain only becomes `degraded` if it was `verified` or already
`degraded`. Otherwise it stays `pending` until it has failed enough times to be
`failed` outright, which is the honest reading of a customer who has not
finished adding their records.

<Callout>
  A warning is not a failure. A domain close to SPF's ten-lookup limit works
  today, and calling that degraded would train people to ignore the state. `warn`
  is treated as a pass by the state machine, which is why the severity of a
  diagnosis code is a decision about the state machine rather than about
  presentation.
</Callout>

## Once per episode

The last piece, and it comes free rather than needing its own rule.

A transition is reported only when the state actually moved. A domain that stays
`degraded` for a week produces one transition, not two thousand.

That is the same shape as "store changes, never observations" from unit 0, and
getting the once-per-episode property out of it (rather than out of a separate
suppression rule somewhere else) is why there is nothing here to forget. There
is no code path that emits an event without a state change, because emitting is
downstream of the change.

## Thirty seconds, then five minutes, then a day

The schedule is adaptive rather than uniform, and this is one of three decisions
that *is* the infrastructure bill rather than merely affecting it.

| State | Interval |
|---|---|
| `pending` / `verifying`, first 15 minutes | 30 seconds |
| `pending` / `verifying`, after that | 5 minutes |
| `verified` | daily, floored at the shortest observed TTL |
| `degraded` | 5 minutes |
| `failed` | hourly |

Sweeping everything on one interval costs roughly ten times as much and gives a
worse experience at both ends: too slow while somebody is waiting at onboarding,
too fast for a domain that has been stable for a month.

These numbers come from the cost model rather than from a measurement, and the
source says so. That is provenance, not a receipt. What would earn the receipt is
the observed distribution of time-to-first-success at onboarding, and of how
often a stable domain's records actually move.

They are injectable rather than environment-tunable, deliberately. A number
nobody has measured does not need a production override: it needs the
measurement. The thresholds that *do* get overrides are the hysteresis ones,
where being wrong means a false alarm reaching a customer rather than a slightly
wasteful poll.

## The TTL floor, and where it does not apply

For a domain that already passes, the only question left is whether anything
changed. Nothing can change faster than the TTL, so polling inside it spends
queries to re-read a cache. Raising the interval to the TTL is free accuracy.

Applying the same floor to a `pending` domain would be actively wrong. There the
customer has just edited their zone and you are waiting to notice; a provider
serving a one-hour negative TTL would push the first re-check an hour out and
make onboarding feel broken.

So the floor applies to `verified` and nothing else. The resolver's own cache may
still delay a pending check by up to the TTL, which is acceptable because it
self-corrects, unlike a scheduling decision, which stays wrong until the state
changes.

That is the whole of regression detection. Consensus for one bad resolver,
counting for one bad minute, a state distinct from failure for a domain that
might recover, and a schedule that spends queries where they buy something.
